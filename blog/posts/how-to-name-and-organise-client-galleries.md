To **organise client photo galleries** well, use a naming system that tells you who the gallery belongs to, what the assignment was and when it happened. Then arrange the photographs in a sequence that makes sense to the client.

A good system should work for a ten-image headshot session and a wedding with thousands of photographs. It should also help another team member find the correct gallery without asking the photographer who created it.

The goal is not to build the most detailed folder structure possible. It is to remove ambiguity. Clear gallery names, useful sections and consistent filenames make delivery easier for the studio and viewing easier for the client.

## Why gallery organisation matters

Disorganised galleries create small problems that accumulate. A client receives a link called "Final Final 2." The studio has three galleries with similar names. Selected images arrive as screenshots because filenames are difficult to identify. Months later, nobody can tell which version was delivered.

A repeatable structure helps you:

- find a gallery quickly
- avoid sending the wrong collection
- separate proofs from finished photographs
- collect selections without confusion
- make downloads easier to understand
- hand work between photographers, editors and client-service staff
- reopen an old job with useful context

Organisation is also part of presentation. Clients may never see your internal folders, but they experience the result when a gallery opens with a clear title, logical sections and files they can identify.

## Start with one naming formula

Choose one formula and use it for every assignment. A practical gallery name is:

**Client name + assignment + date**

For example:

- Ada and Tunde | Wedding | 2026-10-10
- Brightfield Ltd | Executive Headshots | 2026-10-14
- Nwosu Family | Family Portraits | 2026-10-18

Using the year first in the date, as in `2026-10-18`, keeps dates unambiguous and sorts them chronologically. It avoids the confusion between day-month and month-day formats.

If privacy matters, use an internal job code in the public gallery name. The client-facing title can remain warm and readable while your internal record holds the fuller identifying details.

### Add a job number when names may repeat

Client names alone are not always unique. Two clients can share a surname, and one client can book several sessions in the same year.

A job number gives every assignment a stable identifier. For example:

`NDY-2026-0142 | Nwosu Family | Family Portraits`

The number can connect the gallery to the booking, invoice, editing notes and delivery record. Keep it short enough to recognise and never reuse it for another job.

### Avoid vague gallery names

Names such as these are difficult to manage:

- Final Photos
- Wedding Gallery
- Client Delivery
- Edited Pictures
- New Upload

They describe a stage, not a specific assignment. The name may look harmless when there is only one active job, but it becomes unreliable as the studio grows.

## Separate internal names from client-facing titles

Your team and your client need different levels of detail.

An internal name might include a job number, shoot date, photographer and production stage. A client-facing title should be simpler and more personal.

For example:

- **Internal:** `NDY-2026-0142_Nwosu-Family_2026-10-18_FINAL`
- **Client-facing:** `The Nwosu Family Portraits`

Do not expose internal shorthand that the client will not understand. Codes such as `RAW`, `CULL2`, `LR-EXPORT` and `RETOUCH-V3` belong in production storage, not in the polished gallery.

The public title should help the client confirm immediately that they opened the correct collection.

## Use a simple production folder structure

The gallery is only one part of the job. Your working files also need a structure that prevents source files, proofs and final exports from being mixed together.

A practical job folder can contain:

1. `01_Admin`
2. `02_RAW`
3. `03_Selects`
4. `04_Editing`
5. `05_Exports`
6. `06_Client_Gallery`
7. `07_Archive`

Numbered folders remain in the intended order on most systems. The names describe purpose rather than a particular editing application, so the structure can survive a software change.

Keep original files separate from exports. Never place unfinished edits in the same folder that feeds the client gallery. If the job includes several delivery rounds, create clear subfolders such as `Proofs`, `Retouched` and `Final Delivery`.

## Organise the client gallery around the experience

Your internal folders follow production stages. The client gallery should follow the way the client expects to view the assignment.

For a wedding, sections might be:

- Getting Ready
- Ceremony
- Portraits
- Reception
- Details

For a corporate event:

- Venue and Branding
- Speakers
- Audience
- Networking
- Group Photographs

For a portrait session:

- Look 1
- Look 2
- Look 3
- Final Retouched Images

Use section names the client will recognise. Avoid creating so many sections that browsing becomes work. If a section contains only two or three photographs, consider whether it belongs with a broader group.

A strong gallery sequence also needs a beginning and an ending. Choose an inviting cover photograph, open with images that establish the event or session, and finish with a photograph that feels conclusive.

This supports the standards in [what clients expect from a professional photo gallery](/blog/what-clients-expect-from-a-professional-photo-gallery/): clear access, logical presentation and an obvious next step.

## Create filenames that remain useful after download

Once a client downloads a photograph, the gallery title and section may disappear. The filename should still identify the file.

A useful pattern is:

**Job code + client or event + sequence number**

For example:

- `NDY-0142_Nwosu-Family_0001.jpg`
- `NDY-0142_Nwosu-Family_0002.jpg`
- `NDY-0142_Nwosu-Family_0003.jpg`

Use leading zeros so files sort correctly. If the gallery could contain more than 999 images, use four digits from the beginning.

Keep filenames short and safe. Letters, numbers, hyphens and underscores travel well across different devices. Avoid slashes, question marks, emojis and very long descriptions.

Do not rename files after a client has started making selections. If the client sends `NDY-0142_Nwosu-Family_0037.jpg`, that filename should still point to the same photograph in your editing workflow.

## Distinguish proofs from final files

When clients must select favourites before retouching, label the gallery and delivery stage clearly.

Useful stage labels include:

- Proofs for Selection
- Selected Images
- Final Retouched Images
- Album Design Approval

Explain what the client is viewing and what they should do next. A proofs gallery should state:

- how many images the client may select
- how to mark favourites
- the selection deadline
- whether selections can be changed
- how extra images are handled
- when final retouching will be delivered

Do not use a hidden filename convention as the only way to distinguish unfinished photographs. The gallery title and delivery message should make the stage obvious.

## Keep web-size and high-resolution exports clear

If clients receive more than one file size, separate the options and describe their purpose.

For example:

- `High Resolution` for printing and archiving
- `Web Size` for WhatsApp, email and social media

Avoid unexplained terms such as `Large`, `Medium`, `Full` and `Optimised`. They may make sense to the studio but give the client no practical guidance.

If both versions share a filename, keep them in separate download folders. If they must live together, add a consistent suffix such as `_PRINT` or `_WEB`.

Before delivery, download a real file through the client-facing link and confirm that the resolution, colour, filename and compression are correct. ## Add useful information without clutter

The gallery description or delivery message should answer the client's immediate questions. Include:

- what the gallery contains
- whether the files are proofs or final photographs
- what action the client should take
- how to download the files
- whether sharing is allowed
- the gallery expiry date, if any
- where to ask for help

Keep this information concise. A client should not need to read a long policy before viewing their photographs.

For an expiry, write the actual date rather than saying "available for 30 days." A clear date is easier to understand and record.

## Build a gallery status system

Gallery organisation is not only about folders. You also need to know what is happening with each delivery.

Use a small set of status labels, for example:

1. Preparing
2. Ready for Review
3. Proofs Sent
4. Awaiting Selection
5. Retouching
6. Final Gallery Sent
7. Client Confirmed
8. Archived

Define what each status means. "Final Gallery Sent" might mean the delivery message was sent successfully, while "Client Confirmed" means the client opened the gallery and confirmed receipt.

Do not mark a job complete merely because files were uploaded. A professional [gallery delivery workflow](/blog/how-to-deliver-a-photography-gallery-professionally/) includes link testing, client communication and confirmation.

## Decide who can change the gallery

In a team, uncontrolled changes create confusion. Decide who may:

- create a gallery
- rename it
- replace photographs
- change download permissions
- view client selections
- close or archive the gallery

Record important changes. If a team member replaces files after delivery, the studio should know what changed and why.

Before sending the link, one person should complete the final check. That reviewer should use the client-facing link, not the photographer's logged-in view.

## Use one system for selections and editing notes

Selections become difficult to manage when the client sends screenshots, WhatsApp messages and email lists at the same time.

Choose one approved selection method and explain it in the delivery message. Keep image identifiers stable, and record the final selection against the job.

NDYLens supports client galleries where clients can select photographs and leave editing notes, with those choices connected to the client and booking workflow. A connected record reduces the risk of applying a note to the wrong file or losing the final selection in a message thread.

## Archive without losing context

When delivery is complete, archive the job in a way that preserves its meaning.

Keep the job code, client name, assignment date and final gallery name together. Record:

- when the gallery was sent
- when the client confirmed receipt
- the final selection, if applicable
- the gallery expiry date
- where the final exports are stored
- whether albums, prints or other products remain outstanding

Archiving does not replace a backup strategy. Store important files according to your studio's written retention and backup policy, and do not promise permanent storage unless your process genuinely supports it.

## A gallery naming and organisation checklist

Use this checklist before every delivery.

### Naming

- The internal folder has a unique job number
- The client name, assignment and date are correct
- The client-facing gallery title is clear
- No internal editing shorthand is visible
- Filenames are stable and sort in the intended order

### Organisation

- Sections follow the client's viewing experience
- Proofs and final photographs are clearly separated
- Web and high-resolution options are explained
- The cover image and sequence have been reviewed
- Test files, duplicates and unfinished exports are absent

### Delivery

- The client-facing link opens in a private browser window
- Passwords and permissions work as intended
- A real download has been checked
- Selection instructions and limits are clear
- The expiry date and support channel are stated
- The next gallery status is assigned

## Make consistency the standard

The best gallery system is one your studio can use without reinventing it for every job. Start with a clear naming formula, a small set of production folders, client-friendly sections and stable filenames. Document the system, then train everyone who touches a job to follow it.

Consistency makes a growing photography business easier to operate. It also gives every client a gallery that feels deliberate, clear and ready to use.
