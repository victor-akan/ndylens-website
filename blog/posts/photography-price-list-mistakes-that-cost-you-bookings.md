A potential client asks for your photography price list. You send it, they view it and the conversation goes quiet.

It is easy to assume the price was too high. Sometimes that is true. At other times, the client could not tell which option fitted the request, what the amount covered, how the packages differed or what to do after choosing.

A price list is not only a collection of figures. It is a decision tool. It should help the right client understand your offer without forcing them to decode vague names, inconsistent inclusions or hidden conditions.

The most expensive photography price list mistakes are not always dramatic. Small gaps can create doubt at the exact point when a client is deciding whether to continue.

This guide explains the common mistakes, why they can cost bookings and how to correct them without turning your price list into a long sales brochure.

## Mistake 1: Sending the price list before understanding the enquiry

When a person asks, “How much do you charge?”, sending every service and package immediately may feel efficient. The client may receive wedding coverage, portraits, birthdays, commercial work and event packages in one document even though only one service is relevant.

That creates three problems:

- the client has to find the right section alone
- you may recommend an option that does not fit the actual job
- important variables such as date, location, coverage and deliverables remain unknown

Ask only for the information that changes the recommendation. A family portrait enquiry may need the city, preferred date, number of people and type of session. A corporate assignment may require the number of people, location, intended use, delivery deadline and production requirements.

The goal is not to hide pricing or interrogate the client. It is to give the right pricing information in the right context. ## Mistake 2: Using package names that do not explain anything

Names such as Classic, Premium, Gold and Diamond can support your brand, but they do not automatically explain the service. A client should not have to guess whether Gold includes more coverage, more images, an album, an additional photographer or faster delivery.

Add a short “best for” line beneath each name. For example:

- **Portrait Essential:** A focused studio session for one person and one look.
- **Portrait Signature:** More time and variety for two looks or a couple.
- **Portrait Extended:** A broader session for families, personal brands or several setups.

These are examples of structure, not package recommendations. Use names, scopes and inclusions that match the work your studio actually delivers.

If two packages serve almost the same need, consider whether both are necessary. The name cannot solve an offer that lacks a meaningful difference.

## Mistake 3: Listing inclusions in a different order for every package

A client compares options faster when each package follows the same structure. If one begins with coverage time, another begins with prints and a third begins with image count, the reader has to search for matching details.

Choose one comparison order and repeat it. A useful sequence may be:

1. Best for
2. Coverage or session length
3. Locations, outfits or setups
4. Deliverables
5. Delivery timeline
6. Physical products or special inclusions
7. Total price
8. Booking payment

Use the same terms and units across all options. Do not write “two hours” in one package, “half day” in another and “full coverage” in the third unless each term is clearly defined.

Consistency reduces the work required to compare. It also makes the real reason for a price difference easier to see.

## Mistake 4: Making the differences too small or too vague

Three packages may appear different while offering nearly the same experience. If the only visible change is a few extra edited images, the higher price can feel arbitrary.

Identify the differences that materially affect the client or your cost of delivery. Depending on the service, these may include:

- coverage time
- number of locations or setups
- planning and production support
- team size
- number and type of deliverables
- albums, prints or frames
- delivery timing
- commercial usage rights

Put those differences near the top. Do not lengthen the premium package with minor extras simply to make it look more valuable.

If the packages are difficult to separate, revisit the offer itself. Clear design cannot create value distinctions that do not exist.

## Mistake 5: Showing a number without defining the scope

A price is meaningful only when the client knows what it buys. “Event coverage: ₦X” leaves several questions unanswered:

- How many hours are included?
- Is travel included?
- How many photographers will attend?
- What will be delivered?
- When will delivery happen?
- Are prints or albums included?

You do not need to answer every contractual question on the price list. You do need to define the scope that changes the buying decision.

Build the amount from your actual costs, working time, capacity, service scope and profit target. Then present the resulting offer clearly.

## Mistake 6: Using “starting from” without a genuine starting scope

“Starting from” can be honest when there is a defined base service and known variables that increase the final amount. It becomes frustrating when almost nobody can buy the stated starting option or when the client cannot see what it includes.

If you use a starting price, state:

- the base coverage or deliverable
- the conditions included in that price
- the common variables that change it
- when a custom quotation is required

For example, a commercial service may vary by production time, team size, location, quantity and usage. A wedding package may change with coverage hours, travel, extra events or physical products.

Do not use a low starting figure merely to attract a reply and reveal the realistic amount later. That weakens trust even when the final quotation is fair.

## Mistake 7: Hiding the total behind the booking payment

A price list may emphasise the deposit or booking payment while making the total difficult to find. A client can reasonably interpret the most prominent figure as the complete price.

Separate the figures clearly:

```text
Package total: ₦X
Booking payment: ₦Y
Remaining balance: ₦Z
Balance due: [state the actual rule]
```

Replace the placeholders with your real terms. Do not label a payment as a deposit if your agreement uses a different definition. Keep the wording consistent across the price list, quotation, invoice and agreement.

Also explain what confirms the booking. If the date is not reserved until a payment and agreement are completed, say that plainly. Avoid creating urgency with a false hold or implying that interest alone secures availability.

## Mistake 8: Leaving important costs until later

A client may accept the package price and then discover that travel, venue access, additional coverage, rush delivery or a required product costs more.

Not every possible extra belongs on the main price list. Decision-changing costs do.

For each package, identify what is included and what commonly falls outside the scope. A short add-on section can cover items such as:

- extra coverage time
- additional edited images
- another location
- travel outside the included area
- a second photographer
- rush delivery when available
- albums, prints or frames

State the price where it is stable. Where it depends on the request, explain that it will be quoted. Do not present a required component as an optional add-on.

## Mistake 9: Using photography language the client may not understand

Terms such as high resolution, retouching, online gallery, creative direction, usage rights and contact sheet can mean different things to different people.

Use short explanations where misunderstanding could change expectations. For example:

- **Edited images:** Photographs selected, colour-corrected and finished for delivery.
- **Private gallery:** A shareable online space for viewing and downloading the delivered photographs.
- **Planning call:** A conversation to agree the timing, location, outfits and priorities before the shoot.
- **Commercial usage:** The agreed ways a business may use the final photographs.

Keep formal definitions in the agreement where necessary. The price list only needs enough clarity for the client to understand the offer and ask an informed question.

Avoid phrases such as “all pictures” or “all good images” unless your process defines exactly what that means. A realistic quantity or range is usually clearer.

## Mistake 10: Designing for a laptop instead of the client's phone

Many Nigerian clients will receive a rate card through WhatsApp and open it on a phone. A wide comparison table, tiny type or a large file can make a good offer difficult to read.

Test the price list on an ordinary phone before sending it. Check whether:

- the text is readable without constant zooming
- each package can be understood one screen at a time
- the total and major difference appear quickly
- colours have enough contrast
- tables remain usable on a narrow screen
- the file opens without an unreasonable wait
- contact details and links work

A vertical sequence of package cards often works better than a wide table. Keep the same information order in every card so the client can still compare them.

Beautiful design is useful only when it supports comprehension. Decorative fonts, textured backgrounds and long blocks of centred text can make a document look polished while reducing readability.

## Mistake 11: Showing too many packages and add-ons at once

More choice can create more hesitation. If a client has to compare six similar packages and twelve add-ons before understanding the basic service, the price list is doing too much.

Group options by service. Show a manageable set of genuinely different packages, then place common add-ons after the core choice.

If a request regularly needs a custom scope, do not force it into a large menu. Give a suitable starting framework and explain the quotation process.

The right number of packages depends on your business. The test is simple: can the intended client identify the relevant option and explain why it fits?

## Mistake 12: Sending the file without a recommendation

A price list should not replace the conversation. Once you understand the request, recommend the closest option and explain why in one sentence.

For example:

> Based on the six-hour birthday event and the family portraits you described, the Signature Event package is the closest fit because it covers the expected programme without adding the extended team in the larger option.

This does not force the client into that choice. It shows that you listened and gives them a sensible place to begin.

If no package fits, say that a custom quotation is needed. Confirm the variables you will use and give a realistic delivery time for the quotation.

## Mistake 13: Ending with “let me know”

“Let me know” sounds polite, but it does not tell the client what to do next. End with one specific action that matches your real booking process.

Useful actions include:

- Reply with the preferred package and date.
- Ask a question about the option you are considering.
- Confirm the required details so a quotation can be prepared.
- Complete the booking form.
- Approve the quotation and follow the stated payment process.

Do not give several competing actions. The client should know the immediate next step, not every step that may follow.

## Mistake 14: Forgetting the follow-up after sending pricing

Silence does not always mean rejection. A potential client may be comparing options, checking with another decision-maker or waiting for salary or event details.

Record when the price list was sent, which package was recommended and when you will follow up. A useful follow-up refers to the request:

> Hi Amina, I am following up on the family session options I sent on Tuesday. Did you have any questions about the Signature package, or would you like me to explain a smaller option?

Avoid repeated “just checking” messages. Set a reasonable sequence based on the urgency of the job, then close the active enquiry when the sequence ends or the client says no.

## Mistake 15: Reusing an old price list after the business has changed

A price list becomes risky when your costs, service scope, team, delivery time or booking terms have changed but the document has not.

Set a review routine. Check:

- prices against current costs and capacity
- package inclusions against what you actually deliver
- turnaround times against recent workload
- travel boundaries and add-on amounts
- contact details and links
- booking payment and balance rules
- consistency with quotations and agreements

Use a visible version date for internal control. Remove old files from the places your team normally selects them, while preserving any records you need for previous bookings.

## Audit the complete client decision

Reviewing the document line by line is useful, but also test the complete decision. Give the price list to someone who does not work in your studio and provide a simple enquiry scenario.

Ask them to identify:

1. the most relevant package
2. the main difference between the options
3. the total price
4. the booking payment
5. what is not included
6. the next action

If they cannot answer without your explanation, the document still has a clarity problem.

Also review real enquiries. Repeated questions reveal where the price list is weak. If several people ask whether transport is included, move that information. If they confuse the booking payment with the total, separate the figures more clearly. If they ask which package fits, strengthen the “best for” lines and the recommendation message.

## A final photography price list checklist

Before sending your next price list, confirm that:

- you understand enough of the enquiry to send relevant options
- each package says who it is for
- every option follows the same comparison order
- meaningful differences are easy to find
- the scope behind each price is specific
- any starting price has a genuine base offer
- the total and booking payment are clearly separated
- common extra costs are visible
- unfamiliar terms have short explanations
- the layout works on a phone
- the client is not facing too many choices
- your message recommends an appropriate option
- the next action is explicit
- a follow-up date is recorded
- the document matches your current quotation and booking terms

A strong price list will not make every enquiry become a booking. It will help suitable clients understand the offer, compare the right things and decide without avoidable confusion.
