A photography business can look busy and still run short of cash. You may have several confirmed shoots, signed agreements and large outstanding invoices, yet struggle to pay an assistant, replace a damaged memory card or cover transport for the next event.

The problem is often timing. Revenue tells you what the business has earned or agreed to earn. Cash flow shows when money actually enters and leaves the business.

Photography cash flow tracking does not need to become a complicated accounting project. A focused weekly review can show what arrived, what is due, what must be paid and how much cash is genuinely available for decisions.

This guide explains the numbers to track, how to organise them and what to do when the weekly picture reveals pressure.

## Understand the difference between revenue and cash flow

Revenue and cash flow answer different questions.

**Revenue** is the value of photography services and approved additions sold during a period. If a client books a ₦600,000 wedding package, the booking may represent ₦600,000 in revenue under your chosen reporting approach.

**Cash inflow** is the money that has actually reached the business. If that client has paid a ₦200,000 deposit, only ₦200,000 has entered so far.

**Cash outflow** is money leaving the business for assistants, transport, rent, subscriptions, printing, equipment, tax obligations and other costs.

**Net cash flow** is cash received minus cash paid during the period.

A profitable booking can create short-term cash pressure when its delivery costs are due before the client pays the balance. The opposite can also happen: a large deposit creates healthy cash today, although much of it must remain available to deliver a future shoot.

That is why a bank balance alone is not a complete cash flow report. The balance includes money already committed to upcoming work and obligations.

## Set one weekly review time

Choose a consistent day and time. Friday afternoon can work if you want to close the week with a clear position. Monday morning can work if you prefer to plan before new work begins.

The important part is consistency. A 20-minute review every week is more useful than a detailed spreadsheet that is updated only when the business feels stressed.

Use the same review period each time, such as Monday to Sunday. Record the review date and the person responsible. If someone else can update the records, document where payment evidence and cost information should be found.

Before the review begins, reconcile recent transactions against reliable evidence:

- Bank and payment-provider records
- Receipts or transfer confirmations that have been verified
- Booking records and agreed payment schedules
- Supplier invoices and expense receipts
- Refunds, chargebacks or reversed transactions

Do not mark a payment as received merely because a client says it was sent. Confirm it in the relevant account or payment record.

## Track opening cash by account

Start with the cash available at the beginning of the week. List each account the studio uses, rather than writing one figure from memory.

This may include:

- Business bank accounts
- Payment-provider balances awaiting settlement
- A petty-cash balance, if the studio uses one
- A separate tax or reserve account

Avoid mixing personal funds into the operating view. If the owner adds money to the business, record it as an owner contribution rather than photography revenue. If the owner withdraws money, record that separately as well.

Your opening cash should match the previous week's closing cash after any timing differences are resolved.

## Track every cash inflow received this week

Record money when it is verified, and connect each client payment to the correct booking.

Useful fields include:

- Date received
- Client and booking reference
- Service or package
- Payment type, such as deposit, instalment, final balance or add-on
- Amount received
- Payment method
- Account receiving the money
- Processing fee, where applicable
- Net amount settled

Keep non-client inflows separate. Owner contributions, loans, refunds from suppliers and proceeds from selling equipment should not inflate photography sales.

If a payment processor deducts a fee before settlement, record both the gross client payment and the fee. This lets you reconcile the booking correctly while still seeing the cash that reached the account.

## Track cash expected over the next four weeks

Received cash explains what has happened. Expected cash helps you prepare for what comes next.

For every unpaid amount, record:

- Client and booking
- Amount expected
- Contractual or agreed due date
- Current booking stage
- Confidence level
- Next follow-up date
- Person responsible for the follow-up

Do not treat every outstanding invoice as equally reliable. Separate expected inflows into practical groups:

### Confirmed and due

The booking is active, the amount and due date are agreed, and no dispute or change is known.

### Likely but not final

The client may add an album, extra hour, print or another service, but the addition has not been approved and paid for. Keep this outside the committed forecast until it is confirmed.

### Overdue or uncertain

The due date has passed, the client has requested a change, or payment depends on unresolved work. Flag these amounts instead of counting them as dependable cash.

A forecast is not a promise. It is a planning view. Update it when dates, packages or client circumstances change.

## Track every cash outflow paid this week

Use categories that make decisions easier. A long description without a category is difficult to summarise later.

### Job-specific costs

These arise because a particular shoot or delivery is taking place:

- Assistants and second photographers
- Makeup artists or stylists hired for the job
- Travel, accommodation and location fees
- Equipment hired for the booking
- Outsourced editing or retouching
- Albums, prints, frames, packaging and delivery

Connect each one to the relevant booking. This supports both cash planning and later service-profitability analysis. For a fuller comparison, see [how to know which photography service makes the most money](/blog/how-to-know-which-photography-service-makes-the-most-money/).

### Operating costs

These support the business more broadly:

- Studio rent and utilities
- Salaries and regular retainers
- Internet, phone and software subscriptions
- Marketing and advertising
- Insurance and professional services
- Equipment maintenance

### Owner, financing and statutory movements

Keep owner withdrawals, loan repayments and tax payments distinct from operating costs. They still affect cash, but separating them makes the operating picture easier to understand.

For each outflow, record the payment date, payee, category, amount, account, booking reference where relevant and evidence location.

## List upcoming obligations before using the remaining cash

The current bank balance can feel available even when much of it is already spoken for.

Create a four-week obligations list containing:

- Due date
- Supplier or recipient
- Purpose
- Amount
- Whether the amount is fixed or estimated
- Priority
- Account that will pay it

Include shoot costs that must be paid before a client balance arrives. Also include monthly obligations even when no invoice has arrived yet, provided you have a reasonable estimate.

Classify obligations as:

- **Essential:** needed to deliver a confirmed booking, keep the studio operating or meet a formal obligation
- **Important:** valuable and time-sensitive but adjustable with planning
- **Optional:** can be delayed without harming existing client commitments

This classification is not permission to ignore lawful or contractual obligations. It helps identify discretionary spending when timing becomes tight.

## Calculate five weekly cash flow numbers

Once the records are updated, calculate these five figures.

### 1. Total cash received

Add all verified inflows for the week. Keep client payments separate from financing, owner contributions and other non-sales inflows.

### 2. Total cash paid

Add all outflows that cleared during the week. You can split the total into job-specific, operating, owner, financing and statutory categories.

### 3. Net weekly cash flow

**Net weekly cash flow = total cash received minus total cash paid**

A negative week is not automatically a crisis. You may have paid annual insurance or album-production costs while balances are due next week. The trend and the reason matter.

### 4. Closing cash

**Closing cash = opening cash plus total inflows minus total outflows**

Reconcile this figure to the actual accounts. Investigate unexplained differences instead of carrying them into another week.

### 5. Available cash after near-term commitments

**Available cash = closing cash minus essential obligations due before dependable inflows**

This is a planning measure, not a formal accounting standard. Define the period and rules consistently. It gives a more cautious view than the account balance alone.

## Build a simple four-week cash forecast

Create one column for each of the next four weeks and add:

1. Opening cash
2. Dependable expected inflows
3. Essential expected outflows
4. Other planned outflows
5. Net projected movement
6. Projected closing cash

Use due dates, not hopeful assumptions. If a wedding balance is due three days before the event, place it in that week. If an assistant must receive an advance two weeks earlier, place the outflow when it will actually leave.

Run a cautious scenario as well. Ask what happens if the largest expected payment arrives one week late or a shoot requires an unplanned cost. The purpose is not pessimism. It is to reveal whether the business has enough room to respond.

## Watch these warning signs every week

A weekly routine is valuable because it makes changes visible early.

### Overdue balances are growing

If the overdue total rises for several weeks, check whether payment terms are clear, deposits are sufficient and follow-ups happen before the due date. The practical steps in our guide to [reducing late payments from photography clients](/blog/how-to-reduce-late-payments-from-photography-clients/) can help.

### Deposits are funding unrelated work

A deposit for a future wedding may need to cover an assistant, travel, album production or equipment hire for that booking. Spending it elsewhere can create pressure when delivery costs arrive.

### One client dominates expected cash

If the forecast depends on one large payment, show the impact of a delay. Avoid committing optional spending until the payment is verified.

### Fixed costs keep rising faster than dependable inflows

New subscriptions, rent and retainers create recurring obligations. Review whether each cost supports delivery, sales or client experience strongly enough to remain.

### Revenue is increasing but closing cash is falling

Growth may require more upfront production, longer payment terms or heavier operating costs. Compare sales, payment timing and direct costs rather than assuming more bookings will solve the shortage.

## Turn the weekly review into decisions

End every review with specific actions. A report that changes nothing becomes paperwork.

Possible actions include:

- Send a courteous reminder for a due balance
- Confirm a supplier amount before committing to a job
- Move an optional purchase to a later week
- Transfer an agreed amount into a tax or equipment reserve
- Correct a payment recorded against the wrong booking
- Update the forecast after a rescheduled shoot
- Review the deposit or milestone structure for future bookings
- Investigate a cost category that has risen unexpectedly

Assign each action to a person and date. Review unfinished actions at the next weekly meeting.

## Keep the tracker simple enough to maintain

A spreadsheet can support this routine when the business is starting. Use separate areas for transactions, outstanding client balances, upcoming obligations and the four-week forecast. Avoid combining several amounts in one cell or overwriting history when a date changes.

## A weekly photography cash flow checklist

Use this sequence each week:

- Reconcile every business account and unsettled provider balance
- Confirm opening cash matches the prior closing position
- Record and verify client and non-client inflows
- Connect client payments to the correct bookings
- Update balances due and their expected dates
- Record job-specific and operating outflows
- List essential obligations for the next four weeks
- Calculate net cash flow, closing cash and available cash
- Update the four-week forecast and cautious scenario
- Investigate overdue balances and unexplained differences
- Assign actions, owners and deadlines

## Make cash timing visible before it becomes urgent

Good photography cash flow tracking answers three practical questions: what cash is available now, what will probably arrive next and what must leave before then.

Review those answers every week. Keep verified payments separate from promises, connect costs to the work they support and reserve cash for near-term commitments before treating it as spendable.
