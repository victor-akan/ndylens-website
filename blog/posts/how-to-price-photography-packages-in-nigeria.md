Pricing photography packages in Nigeria can feel like guesswork. One photographer charges a certain amount, another offers twice as many images for less, and clients may ask for a discount before they have read the full scope. Copying a competitor's figure looks like a quick solution, but it does not tell you whether the job will pay your costs or reward your time.

A sustainable price starts inside your own business. It reflects what you promise, what it costs to deliver, how many hours the work really takes, how much capacity you have, and the profit you need to maintain and grow the business.

This guide gives you a practical way to work through photography pricing in Nigeria without relying on a universal rate card. The figures in the example are illustrative only. They are not a recommended market rate.

## What a photography package price must cover

A package is more than a number beside a shoot duration. It is a defined service with boundaries. Its price should cover four things:

1. **Direct job costs:** Expenses caused by that particular booking, such as an assistant, transport, studio hire, equipment rental, prints, albums, makeup, delivery or payment processing.
2. **Your working time:** Every hour required to win, prepare, shoot, edit, deliver and close the job, not only the hours spent holding the camera.
3. **A share of business overhead:** Ongoing costs that keep the business available, including power, internet, software, workspace, equipment maintenance, insurance where applicable, marketing and administration.
4. **Profit and reinvestment:** The return left after the cost of delivery and your labour have been accounted for. This is what creates room for emergencies, replacements and growth.

If your package pays the direct expenses but ignores editing time and overhead, a busy month can still leave the business short of cash. Revenue is not the same as profit.

## Step 1: Define exactly what the package includes

You cannot price an unclear promise. Write the scope before calculating the amount.

For each package, specify:

- the type of session or event
- the maximum coverage time
- the number of locations
- the number of photographers or assistants
- the approximate number and format of delivered images
- the editing level included
- the delivery method and expected timeline
- the number of revision rounds, if revisions apply
- any prints, albums, framing or other physical products
- usage rights, particularly for commercial work
- travel area and how work outside it is treated

This protects both you and the client. A client can compare packages on scope rather than on price alone, and you have a written basis for charging when a request goes beyond what was agreed.

Scope also makes your own pricing more accurate. A two-hour portrait session with one location and digital delivery is not the same product as a two-hour brand shoot with several setups, a detailed shot list and commercial usage.

## Step 2: Count the full working time

Shoot duration is only one part of the job. List the entire workflow from first serious enquiry to final delivery.

Your time may include:

- reviewing the enquiry and holding a discovery call
- preparing a quotation, agreement and invoice
- planning the session, location or shot list
- client communication and reminders
- travel, setup and pack-down
- the photography itself
- backing up, selecting and organising files
- retouching and export
- preparing the gallery or delivery folder
- collecting feedback and completing agreed revisions
- following up on outstanding payment
- bookkeeping and archiving

Estimate the hours for each stage, then add them together. Use evidence from completed jobs whenever possible. If the same type of wedding regularly takes 36 total working hours, pricing it as an eight-hour job will hide most of your labour.

Remember that not every hour in a month can be sold. Marketing, equipment care, bookkeeping, learning and responding to enquiries all use capacity. Your billable hours must carry a reasonable share of that non-billable work.

## Step 3: Add direct job costs

Direct costs should be attached to the package that creates them. Build a checklist so small expenses do not disappear.

Common examples in Nigeria include:

- transport, fuel, tolls and parking
- intercity travel or accommodation
- studio or location hire
- an assistant, second photographer or videographer
- rented lenses, lighting, power equipment or backup gear
- makeup artists, stylists or set designers you are supplying
- albums, prints, frames and packaging
- courier or delivery charges
- outsourced editing or design
- payment collection charges

Use current supplier quotes where the cost can change. State how long your own quotation remains valid and what happens if a client changes the date, location or deliverables.

Do not treat a required cost as an optional add-on merely to make the headline price look lower. If an assistant is essential for safe and reliable delivery, include that cost in the relevant package.

## Step 4: Allocate your overhead

Overhead is the cost of being ready to serve a client, even when you are not on a shoot. It often includes:

- workspace or studio expenses
- electricity, backup power and internet
- website, gallery, editing and business software
- phone and communication costs
- marketing and advertising
- accounting, professional fees and licences where applicable
- camera, computer and storage maintenance
- planned equipment replacement
- education and portfolio development

Review the last several months of real spending and calculate a realistic monthly average. Then choose a sensible way to allocate it.

One simple method is:

```text
overhead per billable job = monthly overhead / expected billable jobs that month
```

If your services vary greatly in size, allocate overhead by billable hour instead:

```text
overhead per billable hour = monthly overhead / expected billable hours that month
```

Use expected capacity, not your best month ever. If you can responsibly deliver six jobs of that type, dividing overhead across twelve imaginary bookings will make every package look cheaper than it really is.

## Step 5: Set a value for your labour

Your labour rate should support the income you need from the time the business can genuinely sell. A useful starting calculation is:

```text
labour rate = target monthly owner pay / realistic monthly billable hours
```

This is an internal planning rate, not necessarily a number you show the client. It helps you check whether the package rewards the time required.

Be honest about capacity. A photographer may work many hours in a month but bill only a portion of them directly to clients. If you divide your target pay by every calendar work hour, the rate can be too low to support all the necessary non-billable work.

Review the rate as your skill, demand, responsibilities and costs change. It should not remain frozen simply because it was the first number you used.

## Step 6: Calculate the minimum sustainable price

Once the inputs are clear, use a simple floor:

```text
minimum package price = direct costs + labour value + allocated overhead + target profit
```

The result is a decision tool. It tells you the lowest price that meets the assumptions you entered. Your final quoted price may also reflect complexity, urgency, commercial usage, seasonality, risk or the value of a specialist approach, but it should not ignore the floor.

### An illustrative calculation

Imagine a portrait package with the following internal estimate:

- direct job costs: ₦30,000
- total working time: 10 hours
- internal labour rate: ₦8,000 per hour
- allocated overhead: ₦25,000
- profit and reinvestment target: ₦25,000

The calculation would be:

```text
₦30,000 + (10 x ₦8,000) + ₦25,000 + ₦25,000 = ₦160,000
```

Again, ₦160,000 is an example for explaining the method. It is not a suggested price for a portrait package in Nigeria. Your own scope, costs, capacity, experience and market position determine your result.

Run the calculation for each service instead of applying one margin to everything. ## Step 7: Turn the calculation into clear package tiers

Three tiers can help clients choose without forcing you to create a fresh offer for every enquiry. Each tier should solve a recognisable need and have a clear reason to exist.

For example:

### Essential package

Keep the scope focused. It might use one location, a shorter session, a defined image count and digital delivery. The price still needs to meet your minimum for that scope.

### Signature package

Add the options most clients genuinely value, such as more coverage, a second look, additional edited images or a planning call. Make the extra work and benefit obvious.

### Extended package

Reserve this for clients who need broader coverage, more production support, faster turnaround where feasible, physical products or wider usage. Do not fill it with low-cost extras just to create a higher price.

The difference between tiers should be easy to explain in one sentence. If two packages create almost the same workload, their prices should not be far apart merely because their names differ.

## Step 8: Price add-ons and changes before the client asks

List the common requests that fall outside the base package and calculate them with the same discipline. These may include:

- extra coverage time
- an additional location
- more edited images
- an extra revision round
- rush delivery when capacity allows
- travel beyond the included area
- a second photographer or assistant
- prints, albums or frames
- expanded commercial usage

An add-on should cover the extra cost, time, overhead and profit it creates. A request that sounds small to the client can require backup, selection, editing and delivery time for you.

Define how scope changes are approved. A written change confirmation prevents surprise invoices and keeps the job profitable.

## Step 9: Set deposits and payment milestones

Pricing and payment terms work together. A package can be profitable on paper and still create pressure if you pay suppliers before receiving enough from the client.

Decide:

- what confirms the booking
- when the remaining balance is due
- whether large projects use milestones
- what happens if the date or scope changes
- how cancellations and refunds are handled
- when final delivery becomes available

The correct structure depends on your service and agreement. Make the terms visible before the client commits. Then record each amount consistently. ## Step 10: Test the price with real business data

A calculated price is a starting point, not a permanent answer. Review how each package performs.

Track:

- qualified enquiries received
- quotations accepted and declined
- common reasons prospects give
- average scope changes and add-ons
- total hours actually used
- direct costs actually paid
- revenue collected and still outstanding
- contribution after delivery costs
- client experience and delivery quality

Do not respond to every declined quote by cutting the price. The prospect may not be your ideal client, the value may be unclear, the scope may be wrong, or your follow-up may have been late. Look for a pattern before changing the offer.

## When should you raise a photography package price?

Review your package when:

- direct costs or overhead have risen materially
- delivery consistently takes longer than estimated
- your calendar is full beyond a comfortable capacity
- you have added meaningful skill, equipment or production support
- the package now includes more than it did when priced
- profit is too thin to replace equipment or absorb normal risk
- clients regularly choose it without needing every included item

A price increase is easier to defend when your scope, process and records are clear. Update public price guides, quotation templates and saved replies together so clients do not receive conflicting information.

## Common pricing mistakes to avoid

### Copying another photographer's price

You cannot see their costs, capacity, debt, quality standard or profit target. Competitor research can show how offers are positioned, but it cannot calculate your sustainable price.

### Charging only for shoot time

Planning, travel, editing, delivery and administration are part of the service. Leaving them out does not make them free.

### Offering unlimited deliverables or revisions

Open-ended promises make the workload impossible to estimate. Set a clear allowance and a fair process for additional requests.

### Discounting without reducing scope

If a client's budget is lower, adjust the coverage, deliverables or production requirements. Keeping the full workload while reducing the price simply removes your margin.

### Confusing cash received with profit

A deposit in your account may need to fund assistants, travel, albums or later editing work. Separate committed costs before treating the balance as available income.

### Leaving prices unchanged while costs move

Supplier, transport, power and equipment costs can change. Schedule a regular review and also revisit a package when a major input changes.

## How to communicate your photography package price

Lead with the result and scope, not an apology for the number. A useful quotation should show:

- the client's stated need
- the recommended package
- what is included
- what is not included
- the timeline
- the total price and payment schedule
- the quotation expiry date
- the next step to reserve the date

Keep the language direct. Clients should be able to understand the offer without calling to decode it. If the price is above their budget, offer a smaller defined scope only when it can still meet their essential need and your pricing floor.

## A final photography pricing checklist

Before publishing or sending a package, confirm that you can answer yes to each question:

- Is the service scope precise?
- Have I counted the full working time?
- Are all direct costs current?
- Does the package carry a fair share of overhead?
- Does it pay for my labour?
- Is there room for profit and reinvestment?
- Are add-ons and scope changes priced?
- Are the deposit and payment milestones clear?
- Can I deliver this workload at the expected booking volume?
- Will I review actual results after delivery?

The strongest photography pricing in Nigeria is not the highest or lowest figure in a WhatsApp group. It is a clear, evidence-based price that clients can understand and your business can deliver consistently.
