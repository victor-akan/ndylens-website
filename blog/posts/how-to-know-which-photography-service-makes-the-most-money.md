A wedding package may bring in your biggest payment of the month, but it can also require meetings, travel, a second shooter and several days of editing. A portrait session may have a smaller price, yet take fewer hours and create opportunities for prints or repeat bookings.

So which photography service actually makes the most money?

The answer is not always the service with the highest price. You need to compare revenue with the direct costs, working time, demand and capacity attached to each service. That comparison is the basis of photography service profitability.

This guide gives you a practical way to measure your services using your own booking records. It does not assume that weddings, portraits, corporate work or events are automatically the most profitable. Your numbers should decide.

## Start by defining what "makes the most money" means

Photographers often use revenue, cash and profit as if they mean the same thing. They answer different questions.

### Revenue

Revenue is the amount the client agrees to pay for the service, including approved additions. If a client books a ₦500,000 wedding package and later adds a ₦75,000 album, the current booking revenue is ₦575,000.

Revenue tells you the size of the sale. It does not tell you what remains after delivering it.

### Cash collected

Cash collected is the amount the studio has actually received and verified. A ₦575,000 booking with a ₦200,000 deposit has ₦200,000 collected and ₦375,000 outstanding.

This distinction matters for cash planning. Our guide to [tracking photography payments and outstanding balances](/blog/how-to-track-photography-payments-and-outstanding-balances/) explains how to keep the payment position connected to each booking.

### Contribution after direct costs

For a useful service comparison, subtract the costs that arise because you accepted and delivered that particular job.

**Service contribution = service revenue minus direct service costs**

Direct costs may include:

- A second photographer, assistant or makeup artist hired for the job
- Travel, accommodation, tolls or special logistics
- Venue or equipment hired specifically for the shoot
- Albums, frames, prints, packaging or delivery
- Outsourced editing or retouching
- Payment processing charges attached to the transaction

This figure is not the same as final business profit. Rent, salaries, subscriptions, taxes, insurance, equipment replacement and other overheads still matter. However, contribution gives you a consistent first view of how much each service leaves to cover those wider costs.

## Choose a consistent period for the comparison

One busy month can produce a misleading result. Weddings may cluster in a particular season, corporate portraits may rise near the end of a financial year and family sessions may increase around holidays.

Start with the most recent three to six months if your records are reliable. Use a full year when demand is strongly seasonal or when some services are booked less frequently.

Choose one date basis and use it consistently:

- Shoot date, to compare work delivered during the period
- Booking date, to compare what clients bought during the period
- Payment date, to compare cash received during the period

For service profitability, the shoot date is often a practical starting point because it places the revenue, delivery costs and working time in the same operational period. If a job is not yet complete, mark any cost or time estimate clearly instead of mixing estimates with completed jobs without explanation.

## Group bookings into useful service categories

Your categories should be specific enough to guide decisions but broad enough to produce a meaningful sample.

A studio might use:

- Wedding photography
- Birthday and event coverage
- Family and maternity portraits
- Personal portraits
- Corporate headshots
- Product photography
- School photography
- Albums, prints and other add-ons

Avoid inconsistent labels such as wedding, weddings, bridal and event wedding for the same offer. Clean categories make the totals trustworthy.

If one category contains offers with very different delivery models, split it. A 30-minute studio headshot and a full-day corporate staff session should not automatically be treated as the same service merely because both involve corporate clients.

## Build one service profitability record per completed booking

You can start with a spreadsheet or another structured system. Use one row for each booking and include the fields below.

### Booking and service details

Record:

- Booking reference
- Service category
- Package or scope
- Shoot date
- Booking status
- Lead source, if you want to compare acquisition later

### Revenue and cost details

Record:

- Agreed service revenue
- Verified refunds or discounts
- Direct labour cost
- Direct production cost
- Direct travel and logistics cost
- Other direct cost
- Total direct cost
- Service contribution

Use the final agreed total after approved changes. Do not use only the first quotation if the client added hours, products or another location.

A simple [photography revenue tracker](/blog/a-simple-revenue-tracker-for-photography-businesses/) can provide the booking and payment foundation for this analysis.

### Time details

Record the hours spent on:

- Enquiry, consultation and planning
- Travel and setup
- Shooting
- Culling and editing
- Client review and revisions
- Gallery preparation and delivery
- Album, print or other fulfilment
- Payment follow-up and administration

Do not aim for minute-by-minute precision at the beginning. A consistent estimate is more useful than no time record at all. Round to the nearest half hour and improve your tracking over the next few jobs.

## Calculate four measures for every service

No single measure gives the whole answer. Use at least these four.

### 1. Total service revenue

Add the agreed revenue from completed bookings in each category.

**Total service revenue = sum of revenue for the service**

This shows which service brings the most sales value across the period. A category can lead here because its price is high, its booking volume is high or both.

### 2. Contribution per booking

First calculate the contribution for every job, then find the average for the category.

**Average contribution per booking = total service contribution divided by completed bookings**

This helps compare a high-value, low-volume service with a lower-value service sold more often.

### 3. Contribution margin

Contribution margin shows the percentage of service revenue remaining after direct costs.

**Contribution margin = service contribution divided by service revenue, multiplied by 100**

For example, a service that produces ₦400,000 in revenue and ₦100,000 in direct costs has a ₦300,000 contribution and a 75% contribution margin.

A higher margin is useful, but it does not automatically make that service the best choice. A high-margin service with very little demand may contribute less to the business overall.

### 4. Contribution per working hour

Divide the service contribution by the total hours required to sell, prepare, shoot, edit and deliver the work.

**Contribution per hour = service contribution divided by total working hours**

This exposes offers that look attractive by price but consume more of the studio's limited time than expected.

## A practical comparison example

Imagine that a studio reviews three completed service categories over six months:

| Service | Bookings | Revenue | Direct costs | Contribution | Total hours | Contribution per hour |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Weddings | 6 | ₦3,600,000 | ₦1,080,000 | ₦2,520,000 | 300 | ₦8,400 |
| Portraits | 24 | ₦2,880,000 | ₦432,000 | ₦2,448,000 | 168 | ₦14,571 |
| Corporate headshots | 8 | ₦2,400,000 | ₦480,000 | ₦1,920,000 | 112 | ₦17,143 |

In this example, weddings generate the most revenue and the highest total contribution. Corporate headshots produce the highest contribution per working hour. Portraits sit between them and provide the highest booking volume.

There is no single universal winner. The right conclusion depends on the studio's goal:

- To maximise total contribution, weddings currently lead
- To make better use of limited hours, corporate headshots lead
- To maintain booking frequency and a broad client base, portraits may remain strategically valuable

These figures are illustrative, not industry benchmarks. Replace them with your own verified records before changing your prices or marketing.

## Add demand and capacity to the financial view

A profitable service is only useful if clients want it and the studio can deliver it well.

### Measure real demand

For each service, review:

- Qualified enquiries received
- Bookings confirmed
- Enquiry-to-booking conversion rate
- Cancellations or postponements
- Average wait between enquiry and booking
- Seasonal changes in demand

A service with excellent contribution per hour may still struggle if very few suitable clients request it. Another service may deserve attention because demand exists but weak follow-up or unclear packaging limits conversion.

### Measure practical capacity

Consider:

- How many jobs the team can deliver without quality dropping
- Whether the service depends on one photographer or specialist
- Editing and delivery workload after the shoot
- Equipment, studio and travel constraints
- Whether busy dates conflict with other valuable services

Capacity explains why adding more bookings is not always the best route to more profit. If every extra event creates an editing backlog, pricing, scope or production processes may need attention before demand is increased.

## Look beyond the first booking

Some services create value after the initial job.

A maternity session may lead to newborn, dedication, birthday and family sessions. Corporate headshots may become an annual team update. A wedding client may later return for anniversary or family photography.

Track repeat bookings and relevant add-ons by original service when your records allow it. Do not assign future revenue to the first booking until it has actually happened. Instead, treat repeat activity as a separate signal of long-term service value.

Useful questions include:

- What percentage of clients in this category book again?
- Which service produces the most relevant referrals?
- Which offers lead naturally to albums, prints or additional sessions?
- How long does it take for the next booking to happen?

This is one reason connected client history matters. A profitable first job is valuable, but a well-served client relationship can create additional opportunities over time.

## Include overhead before making major decisions

Contribution analysis helps compare services, but the business still has shared costs.

List monthly overhead such as:

- Studio rent and utilities
- Permanent salaries or regular retainers
- Software and communication tools
- Insurance and professional services
- Marketing costs that are not tied to one booking
- Equipment maintenance and planned replacement

You can review whether total contribution across all services covers these costs and leaves an acceptable operating result. If you allocate overhead to individual services, document the method and use it consistently. A professional accountant can help you choose an approach suited to your business and reporting obligations.

Do not remove a service solely because one rough calculation looks weak. Check the records, sample size, seasonality, strategic role and whether the service is priced or delivered poorly.

## Use the findings to improve your offers

Once the comparison is reliable, choose a specific action for each service.

### Keep and promote

The service has healthy demand, contribution and capacity. Improve visibility, show relevant portfolio work and make the booking path clear.

### Reprice

Demand exists, but the contribution per booking or hour is too low. Review scope, direct costs, revision limits, travel rules and package price.

### Standardise

The service is profitable but operationally inconsistent. Use a repeatable checklist, package structure and delivery workflow.

### Add capacity carefully

The service performs well but the team cannot accept more work. Consider scheduling changes, outsourcing or hiring only after checking quality and cost.

### Test before expanding

The numbers look promising but the sample is small. Run a defined test with a target number of bookings and review the results again.

### Pause or reposition

The service has weak demand and poor contribution even after reasonable improvements. Reduce promotion, change the target client or pause the offer while protecting existing commitments.

## Review profitability without losing the client experience

Efficiency should not mean rushing clients or quietly removing promised work.

If editing, revisions or client communication take longer than planned, investigate why. The answer may be a clearer scope, better preparation, improved selection process or a price that reflects the actual work.

The goal is to design services that are financially sustainable and consistently delivered. That protects both the studio and the client experience.

## A monthly photography service profitability checklist

At the end of each month:

- Confirm completed bookings and consistent service categories
- Update final revenue, refunds and approved discounts
- Record direct labour, production and travel costs
- Estimate total working hours for each completed job
- Calculate contribution, margin and contribution per hour
- Review demand, conversion and capacity constraints
- Note repeat bookings and add-ons separately
- Investigate unusual results or missing records
- Choose one improvement to test before the next review

## Let your own records choose the winner

The most profitable photography service is not automatically the one with the largest invoice. It is the service that produces strong contribution after direct costs, uses working time well, has enough demand and fits the studio's capacity.

Measure total revenue, contribution per booking, contribution margin and contribution per hour. Then add demand, repeat potential and operational reality before acting.
