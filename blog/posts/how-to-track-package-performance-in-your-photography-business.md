Photography package performance tells you whether each offer is attracting the right enquiries, becoming confirmed bookings and contributing enough to justify the work required. It gives you a clearer basis for deciding which packages to promote, revise, reprice or retire.

Without that record, it is easy to judge a package by the wrong signal. A premium wedding package may produce the largest single payment but require more meetings, travel, assistants and editing days. A portrait package may bring in less per booking while using fewer hours and creating more repeat opportunities. A package that receives many enquiries may still convert poorly because its scope is unclear.

The useful question is not simply, “Which package sold most?” It is, “What happened from enquiry to delivery, and what did the package contribute after the work and direct costs were considered?”

This guide shows how to track that answer with practical records a solo photographer or small studio can maintain.

## What photography package performance should measure

A package has several jobs. It should help a suitable client understand the offer, make a confident booking decision, receive the promised service and leave enough value for the business after delivery.

That means performance needs more than one number. Review each package through five connected views:

1. **Demand:** How many suitable people enquired about it?
2. **Conversion:** How many qualified enquiries became confirmed bookings?
3. **Revenue:** How much was booked, collected and still outstanding?
4. **Contribution:** What remained after the direct costs caused by those bookings?
5. **Workload:** How much time and capacity did the package use?

Client experience and delivery quality matter too. A package is not successful if it meets a revenue target only because the studio is overworked or routinely misses what was promised.

Use the measures together. One metric can reveal a problem, but it rarely explains the whole story.

## Define every package before comparing results

You cannot compare packages accurately when their names hide different scopes.

For each active package, record:

- package name and version
- service category
- price during the period
- included coverage time
- locations, looks or setups included
- deliverables and approximate image count
- editing level
- physical products, if any
- included team members
- delivery timeline
- usage rights where relevant
- common add-ons
- date the version became active

Package versions are important. If you raise the price, change the image allowance or remove an album, the updated offer is not identical to the earlier one. Keep the version or effective date so a result from March is not compared carelessly with a different scope sold in November.

Also separate a standard package from a heavily customised quotation. If a corporate headshot job began with your standard offer but later added another location, extra staff and expanded usage, report the final scope honestly. Otherwise the base package may appear to earn more without showing the work that created the difference.

## Choose a consistent reporting period

Start with a monthly review and add a quarterly view when you have enough records. A week may be too short for services with long sales cycles, while a full year can hide a recent change in price or demand.

Use one date rule for each report:

- **Enquiry reports:** group by the date the enquiry arrived.
- **Booking reports:** group by the date the booking was confirmed.
- **Cash reports:** group by the date payment was verified.
- **Delivery reports:** group by the date the job was completed or delivered.

These totals will not always match in the same month. A wedding confirmed in November may be photographed in February and paid through several instalments. That is normal. Label each report clearly rather than forcing every event into one date.

## Track the package from enquiry to outcome

Every serious enquiry should have one record that stays connected to its result.

Capture at least:

- client or company name
- enquiry date and source
- service requested
- package discussed or recommended
- quoted price and any approved change
- current stage
- final outcome
- reason closed, when known
- booking confirmation date
- amount booked
- payments collected
- outstanding balance
- direct delivery costs
- working hours
- add-ons purchased

If the person compares two packages, record the package you recommended and the one they eventually chose. That helps you see whether clients regularly move up, move down or ask for a combination your current offer does not explain well.

Do not guess why an enquiry closed. Use “reason unknown” when the person did not tell you. A tidy but invented reason will lead to the wrong decision later.

## Measure demand without counting every message as a lead

Demand begins with qualified interest, not views, reactions or casual price requests that lack a real need.

Define a qualified enquiry for your studio. It may require a recognisable service, a plausible date or timeline, a location and enough information to decide whether the work fits. Apply the same rule across packages.

Then calculate:

```text
qualified demand for a package = qualified enquiries linked to that package
```

Compare demand with exposure. A package promoted on your website, price guide and social channels will naturally receive more attention than one mentioned only during calls. Low enquiry volume may mean low demand, but it may also mean clients rarely see the offer.

Useful questions include:

- Was the package actively promoted during the period?
- Did the right type of client see it?
- Was the scope easy to understand?
- Did enquiries repeatedly ask for something the package excludes?
- Did seasonality affect the service?

Record the answer before changing the price.

## Calculate booking conversion consistently

Package conversion shows how often qualified opportunities become confirmed work.

```text
package booking conversion rate = confirmed bookings / qualified enquiries x 100
```

Use your studio's real confirmation rule. A positive message or promise to pay is not automatically a booking. Count the job only after the client completes the stated confirmation requirements.

Conversion needs context. A package with four qualified enquiries and two bookings has a 50% conversion rate, but the sample is still small. One additional booking would change the percentage sharply. Show the counts beside the rate and avoid major decisions from a short or unusual period.

When conversion is lower than expected, review the full path:

- Did the package match the enquiry?
- Was the quotation sent promptly?
- Were price and deliverables clear?
- Did the client receive a useful follow-up?
- Was the requested date available?
- Did the final quote move far beyond the package shown?

A conversion problem is not always a price problem.

## Separate booked revenue, collected cash and outstanding balances

These three figures answer different questions.

**Booked revenue** is the agreed value of confirmed work during the period. **Collected cash** is money you verified as received. **Outstanding balance** is the remaining amount still expected under the agreement.

For a package:

```text
booked revenue = sum of confirmed booking values
```

```text
collected cash = sum of verified payments received
```

```text
outstanding balance = agreed booking value - verified payments - approved reductions or refunds
```

Do not call a deposit profit. Part of it may need to fund transport, assistants, rentals, albums or later editing work. Similarly, a large outstanding balance should not be treated as available cash.

## Calculate contribution after direct costs

Revenue shows the size of a sale. Contribution shows what remains after the costs caused directly by delivering it.

Direct costs may include:

- assistants or second photographers
- studio or location hire
- transport, tolls and accommodation
- equipment rental
- outsourced retouching or design
- albums, prints, frames and packaging
- courier or delivery charges
- job-specific payment processing charges

Use:

```text
package contribution = package revenue - direct package costs
```

This is not necessarily final accounting profit. It does not automatically include all overhead, taxes, owner pay or equipment replacement. It is a practical operating measure for comparing offers on the same basis.

You can also calculate:

```text
contribution margin = package contribution / package revenue x 100
```

Use the same cost definition for every package. If transport is included for weddings but ignored for portraits, the comparison will be misleading.

## Track the full working time

Two packages can produce the same contribution and still affect the business very differently.

Record time spent on:

- enquiry and sales communication
- planning and preparation
- travel, setup and pack-down
- photography
- backup and selection
- editing and retouching
- client review and revisions
- gallery preparation or product fulfilment
- payment follow-up and administration

Then calculate:

```text
contribution per working hour = package contribution / total hours used
```

This measure is useful for capacity decisions. A package with a lower total contribution may still be attractive if it uses much less time and fits around larger jobs. A high-revenue package may need revision if it repeatedly occupies more hours than the price assumes.

Time records do not need to be perfect to become useful. Start with honest estimates after every completed job, then improve the method as patterns become visible.

## Review add-ons, upgrades and scope changes

A package may perform well because clients regularly add products or coverage. Track that separately instead of attributing the whole amount to the base offer.

Record:

- base package value
- add-on type
- add-on revenue
- extra direct cost
- extra working time
- whether the add-on was planned or requested later

This reveals whether an add-on is genuinely helpful or merely repairs a package that starts with too little scope.

Also review how clients move between tiers. If most enquiries ask for the middle package and then remove one feature, the package may need a clearer boundary. If many clients start with the smallest offer and consistently add the same item, that item may belong in a revised tier.

## Turn the review into a clear action

At the end of each monthly or quarterly review, give every package one decision:

### Keep

The package attracts suitable clients, converts reasonably, delivers an acceptable contribution and fits your capacity. Continue tracking it without changing several variables at once.

### Improve the explanation

Demand exists, but clients misunderstand the difference between tiers or repeatedly ask the same question. Rewrite the scope, comparison or recommendation before changing the price.

### Reprice

Actual direct costs or working hours are consistently higher than the assumptions behind the price. Recalculate the package using current evidence and update every place the old price appears.

### Redesign

The package includes items clients rarely value, creates repeated scope changes or does not fit how the studio delivers the work. Adjust the offer, then treat the new version separately in future reports.

### Promote

The package performs well but receives little exposure. Give it a clear place in your portfolio, price guide, enquiry responses and relevant marketing.

### Retire

The offer has weak demand, poor contribution, operational problems or too much overlap with another package, and a reasonable improvement has not solved the issue. Close it to new enquiries while honouring confirmed work.

Change one major element at a time where possible. If you alter the price, scope, name and sales message together, it becomes difficult to learn what caused the next result.

## Avoid common tracking mistakes

### Comparing package prices instead of final booking values

Customisation and add-ons can change the real amount. Keep the base price and final agreed value visible.

### Treating every enquiry as equally qualified

Different package teams may apply different standards. Write one qualification rule and use it consistently.

### Ignoring closed enquiries

Only reviewing bookings hides what potential clients declined, misunderstood or could not schedule.

### Using cash received as the only performance measure

Payment timing can make one month look stronger than another even when the underlying bookings were confirmed earlier.

### Forgetting delivery costs and time

High sales can conceal a package that is expensive or exhausting to deliver.

### Mixing package versions

A new price or scope needs an effective date so the old and new results can be separated.

### Reacting to one unusual booking

Show counts, note outliers and review several periods before making a major decision.
