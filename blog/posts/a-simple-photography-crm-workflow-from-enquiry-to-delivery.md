A photography client rarely experiences your business as separate tasks. To them, the enquiry, booking, shoot and delivery are one continuous experience. When that journey is organised, the studio feels calm and professional. When it is not, even excellent photographs can be surrounded by slow replies, missed details and uncertain next steps.

A photography CRM workflow gives every job a clear path from the first message to the final follow-up. It shows you where the client is now, what has already happened and what must happen next.

This guide lays out a simple workflow you can adapt for portraits, weddings, birthdays, family sessions, corporate work and other photography services.

## What a useful photography CRM workflow should do

The purpose of a workflow is not to create more administrative work. It is to reduce the number of decisions you have to remake for every client.

A useful workflow should help you answer five questions quickly:

1. Who is this client?
2. What have they requested or booked?
3. What stage has the job reached?
4. What needs to happen next?
5. Who is responsible for that action?

Each stage should represent a meaningful change in the job. A client should move forward because an action has been completed, not simply because time has passed.

If your information is still spread across notebooks, chats and spreadsheets, complete a basic [photography CRM setup](/blog/how-to-move-your-photography-business-from-notes-to-a-crm/) before building a detailed pipeline. A clean client record is the foundation of the workflow.

## The simple workflow at a glance

A practical starting point is:

1. New enquiry
2. Enquiry qualified
3. Quote or package sent
4. Awaiting confirmation
5. Booking confirmed
6. Shoot preparation
7. Shoot completed
8. Editing and client selection
9. Ready for delivery
10. Delivered
11. Follow-up due

You can combine stages if your service is straightforward. A passport photographer, for example, may not need the same preparation stages as a wedding studio. The best workflow is the smallest one that keeps the work clear.

## Stage 1: Capture every new enquiry

The workflow starts when someone asks about your services. That enquiry may arrive through WhatsApp, Instagram, email, a phone call, a referral or a website form.

Create one record as soon as the conversation becomes a genuine business enquiry. Capture the essentials:

- Client name
- Phone number and email address
- Enquiry source
- Photography service required
- Preferred date
- Location, if known
- How the client found you
- A short summary of what they need

Do not make the first step so demanding that you postpone it. You can add details later. At this point, your main goal is to prevent a promising enquiry from disappearing into a busy inbox.

### Set a next action immediately

Every enquiry should have one next action. It might be:

- Reply with your availability
- Ask for the event location
- Send a package guide
- Arrange a consultation
- Follow up tomorrow

Give that action a due date. A stage without a next action becomes a waiting room where leads are easily forgotten.

## Stage 2: Qualify the enquiry

Qualification means confirming that you understand the request and can realistically serve it. You do not need a long questionnaire for every job. Ask only what affects availability, pricing or delivery.

For a birthday portrait session, you may need the date, number of people, number of looks and preferred studio or outdoor location. For a wedding, you may need the traditional and white wedding dates, locations, expected coverage hours and required deliverables. For corporate work, the client may need to confirm the number of staff, usage and deadline.

Update the record with the answers rather than leaving important details buried in chat messages. Then decide whether the enquiry should move forward, remain on hold or be closed.

Closing an unsuitable enquiry is not a failure. It keeps your pipeline honest and gives you a clear record if the client returns later.

## Stage 3: Send the right package or quote

Once the request is clear, send the appropriate package, estimate or quote. Record:

- What you sent
- The date it was sent
- The quoted amount
- The validity period, if applicable
- Included services and deliverables
- Any important assumptions
- The date you plan to follow up

The CRM record should summarise the commercial agreement. You should not have to search through weeks of messages to remember whether extra coverage, transport or an album was included.

Avoid moving the job to **Awaiting confirmation** without scheduling a follow-up. The client may be comparing photographers, checking dates with family members or waiting for internal approval. A polite, timely follow-up is part of the sales process.

## Stage 4: Confirm the booking

Define exactly what makes a booking confirmed in your studio. For many photographers, confirmation happens when the client accepts the terms and pays the required deposit. Whatever your rule is, apply it consistently.

Before moving the job to **Booking confirmed**, check that the record includes:

- Confirmed date and time
- Confirmed location
- Selected package or agreed scope
- Total fee
- Deposit received
- Outstanding balance
- Payment due date
- Client contact details
- Any contract or acceptance required

Do not treat verbal interest as a confirmed booking if another client could request the same date. A clear status protects both your calendar and the client's expectations.

The CRM should make the difference between an enquiry and a confirmed job visible. This is one reason a structured system can help a [solo photographer run a more professional studio](/blog/how-a-crm-helps-a-solo-photographer-run-a-professional-studio/), even without a large team.

## Stage 5: Prepare for the shoot

Move confirmed jobs into preparation early enough to avoid a last-minute rush. The preparation checklist will vary by service, but it may include:

- Confirming the venue or studio
- Agreeing on a mood board or shot list
- Collecting clothing, colour or styling preferences
- Confirming the number of people or looks
- Assigning a second shooter or assistant
- Booking equipment or transport
- Sending a reminder
- Confirming the outstanding payment plan
- Recording special access or delivery requirements

Keep the checklist attached to the booking rather than relying on memory. For a Lagos event, details such as access time, traffic route, parking and venue restrictions can affect when the team needs to leave.

### Use one owner for every task

If several people work on a job, give each action one clear owner. "The team will handle it" is not an assignment. One person can still collaborate with others, but everyone should know who is responsible for completing and updating the task.

## Stage 6: Complete the shoot record

When the session or event ends, update the CRM while the details are still fresh. Record that the shoot is complete and note anything that affects the next stage.

Useful post-shoot details include:

- Actual shoot date
- Work completed
- Any additional service requested
- Outstanding payment
- Expected selection or delivery date
- Storage location or job reference
- Important client feedback
- The person responsible for editing

Do not overload the client record with technical editing notes that belong in your production system. Keep enough context to manage the client and delivery accurately.

If the client requested extra photographs, coverage or products on the day, document the agreement before editing begins. This reduces disagreement when the final balance is due.

## Stage 7: Manage selection and editing

Some photographers deliver a curated final set. Others ask the client to choose images before editing. Your workflow should reflect the method you actually use.

For client selection, track:

- Gallery or selection link sent
- Date sent
- Number of selections included
- Selection deadline
- Whether editing notes were received
- Follow-up date if the client has not selected

For editing, track the status rather than every technical adjustment. Useful production statuses may include **Queued**, **In editing**, **Quality check** and **Complete**.

## Stage 8: Prepare for delivery

Before marking a job ready, complete a short quality check. Confirm that:

- The correct files are included
- The agreed number of photographs is ready
- File names and folders are clear
- The gallery or delivery link opens correctly
- Access settings are appropriate
- The outstanding balance has been handled according to your policy
- The client name and contact channel are correct

This stage matters because a technical mistake at delivery can undo the confidence built throughout the job. Open the client-facing link yourself, preferably in a private browser window, before sending it.

## Stage 9: Deliver and record the handover

When the photographs are sent, record the delivery date and method. The client should receive a clear message explaining what has been delivered and any action they need to take.

Your delivery record may include:

- Delivery date
- Delivery channel
- Gallery or file link
- Download or access instructions
- Any remaining physical product
- Outstanding balance, if one remains
- Date to check that the client received everything

NDYLens supports gallery delivery and can send photographs through WhatsApp. Keep the delivery status accurate so that the studio can distinguish work that is technically complete from work that is still awaiting client confirmation or a physical item.

Do not mark a job delivered merely because the export finished. Delivery means the client has been given access through the agreed channel.

## Stage 10: Follow up after delivery

The workflow should not end the moment the files are sent. A simple post-delivery follow-up can confirm that the client received the photographs and create a natural opportunity to continue the relationship.

Your follow-up may include:

- Confirming that the client can access the files
- Resolving any delivery question
- Asking for feedback at an appropriate time
- Requesting a review or referral when suitable
- Recording an anniversary, birthday or other relevant future date
- Setting the next relationship reminder

That turns delivery into the beginning of retention, rather than the point where the client disappears into an old chat.

## The fields that keep the workflow useful

You do not need a complicated database. For most active jobs, the following fields provide enough control:

- Client
- Service
- Booking stage
- Shoot date
- Total fee
- Amount paid
- Outstanding balance
- Next action
- Action due date
- Task owner
- Delivery date
- Follow-up date

You can add service-specific fields later. Start by making these core fields complete and consistently used.

If the purpose of the system still feels unclear, begin with [what a photography CRM is](/blog/what-is-a-photography-crm/) and identify the decisions you want it to make easier.

## A weekly CRM review for photographers

Set aside fifteen to thirty minutes each week to review the pipeline. Look for:

- New enquiries without a response
- Quotes without a follow-up date
- Interested clients who have not confirmed
- Upcoming shoots with incomplete preparation
- Jobs waiting for selection or editing
- Completed edits that have not been delivered
- Outstanding balances
- Delivered jobs without a follow-up
- Records with no next action

This review is where the CRM becomes a management tool. It helps you see problems early instead of discovering them when a client asks for an update.

## Common workflow mistakes

### Using vague stages

Labels such as **Pending** or **In progress** do not explain what is waiting or who must act. Use stages that point to a clear next step.

### Creating too many stages

If every small task becomes a pipeline stage, the workflow becomes difficult to maintain. Keep detailed tasks in checklists and reserve stages for meaningful changes in the client journey.

### Moving records without completing the action

A client should enter **Booking confirmed** only when your confirmation conditions are met. Accurate stages are more valuable than an impressive-looking pipeline.

### Leaving finished jobs in active production

Move delivered work out of production and into follow-up. This keeps your current workload visible while preserving the relationship history.

### Recording status without a next action

Status tells you where the job is. The next action tells you how it moves. Most active records need both.

## A simple rule for every client record

At any point, you should be able to open a booking and understand the situation in less than a minute. You should see what the client requested, what was agreed, what has been paid, what stage the work has reached and what happens next.

That is the real benefit of a photography CRM workflow. It replaces scattered memory with a repeatable client experience, while leaving you more attention for the photographs themselves.
